File preparation

Eight rules that save a reprint.

Riso is not a digital press. Each colour is a separate stencil burned from a black and white image, so we need your file split the way the machine thinks. Follow these and preflight passes first time.

  1. One greyscale layer per colour

    Send each ink as its own page or layer in pure greyscale. Black in the file is 100% ink, white is bare paper. Name the layers after the drum: 01-blue.pdf, 02-pink.pdf.

  2. Keep tints between 10% and 85%

    Below 10% the stencil drops out to nothing; above 85% the ink floods and looks the same as solid. A 30% blue over a 30% pink is where the good stuff lives.

  3. 300 dpi, exported as .pdf

    Bitmaps at 300 dots per inch at final size. Vector shapes and live text are fine. No screen-colour files, no four-colour process, no spot channels.

  4. 3 mm bleed, 5 mm safe zone

    Add 3 mm past the trim edge and keep anything important 5 mm inside it. The feed rollers drift a millimetre or two on every sheet.

  5. Expect 1 to 2 mm of misregistration

    Colours will not sit exactly on top of each other, and thin outlines that rely on it will look broken. Design so a slip reads as charm, or trap it with a knockout.

  6. Leave a 10 mm paper margin on the feed edge

    The rollers grip the top of the sheet and leave marks in heavy ink there. Keep the short top edge light, or plan for us to trim it.

  7. No large solid floods on the reverse

    Two-sided jobs with big solids on both faces will smear because riso ink never fully cures. Keep one side lighter than the other.

  8. Send a colour mock-up too

    A flattened screen image of how you imagine it, with the ink names written on. It is the only way we can check your separations are the right way round.

Honest bit

What riso does well, and what it does badly.

A risograph is a 1980s stencil duplicator with beautiful ink. It is cheap, fast and vivid, and it is also messy, imprecise and allergic to some kinds of artwork. Read both columns before you send us a job.

Good at

say yes to
  • Flat, punchy colourFluo pink and blue cannot be reproduced by an inkjet. The ink sits on the paper rather than in it.
  • Short runs at long-run pricesFifty posters for £58 is not a typo. A master costs pennies and the ink is cheap.
  • Halftones and grainPhotographs converted to a coarse dot or stochastic grain look better on riso than on any other press.
  • Overprints as free coloursTwo drums give you three colours, three give you six or seven. Layers are the whole trick.
  • Low footprintSoy ink, no heat, no solvent, no plate chemistry, and recycled paper goes through fine.

Bad at

talk to us first
  • Tight registrationColours drift 1 to 2 mm. Outlined text and fine multi-colour logos will look drunk.
  • Large solid floodsBig areas of 100% ink go blotchy, roller-marked and never quite dry. Tints under 85% behave far better.
  • Exact brand coloursThere are twelve inks. None of them is your corporate teal, and mixing does not work like paint.
  • Smudge-proof finishesRiso ink stays soft. It rubs onto fingers and facing pages, especially on coated or glossy stock, which we will not run.
  • Photo-real colourSkin tones, gradients and full-colour photography come out as posterised art prints, not proofs. Sometimes that is lovely. Sometimes not.
