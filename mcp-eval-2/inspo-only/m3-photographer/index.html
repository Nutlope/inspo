An introduction, in one paragraph

I make portraits of people who would rather be doing something else — chefs at the end of a service, swimmers in the half-hour before a race, novelists on the way to the post office. Between commissions I take the long road home and come back with travel work: a notebook of light, dust, and the conversations you can only have at the side of a road.

Approach  ·  A short essay

How I work, in three notes.

On portraiture

I think a portrait is really a conversation that one person forgot to keep up. My job is to ask quiet questions for the first twenty minutes and then forget I have a camera for the next forty. The sittings run long because the good frames live somewhere between the second cup of coffee and the moment you stop thinking about your hands.

I shoot mostly on medium-format film and finish on a digital back when light won't wait. I never retouch the shape of a face; I do retouch tired skin if the editor wants it.

On travel

Travel work, for me, is a slower kind of portraiture. The subject just happens to be a road, a kitchen, a coastline, a hotel lobby at three in the afternoon. I file the road the way I'd file a person — with a name, a date, and a small note about what we talked about on the way.

I drive between commissions when there's time. The longest piece I've made started as a wrong turn outside Évora and ended as fourteen pages in The Gentlewoman two years later.

On commissions

I take six to eight editorial commissions a year and a handful of private portrait sittings each season. Budgets vary; the brief should be specific about subject, format, and the kind of likeness you're after. I'm represented for advertising work by Atelier Mira (New York / London).

For everything else — including festivals, prints, and the occasional wedding of a friend — write directly. Replies within two working days.
