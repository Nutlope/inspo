Thrown, glazed and fired in one room

Five pieces on the shelf

Everything is thrown, trimmed, glazed and fired here. When a run sells out it stays out until the next firing, which is a calendar the kiln keeps, not us.

  1. Alfama bowl

    Deep enough for soup, wide enough for rice. The glaze pools darker where the wall turns.

  2. Tejo plate

    A dinner plate with a low rim, so a knife has somewhere to go and nothing slides off.

  3. Bica cup

    Small, unhandled, sized for the coffee you drink standing at the counter.

  4. Graça jug

    Water, wine, or a fistful of stems carried home from the market wrapped in paper.

  5. Estufa dish

    Oven to table, then straight into the middle of everything. Thick base, honest weight.

The kiln sets the calendar, not the shop

Clay decides the week. A piece thrown on Monday is not finished until it has been trimmed, dried slowly enough not to crack, fired once bare, glazed, and fired again. None of that can be hurried by wanting it sooner, which is why the shelf is never full and never quite empty.

We make what we use. If a shape does not survive a month of our own breakfasts, it does not go on the shelf. That is the whole quality process: it is slower than a spreadsheet and it has never once been wrong.

Small batches mean the pieces differ. Two Alfama bowls from the same firing will not be identical twins, and the one you take home is the one you looked at.

A weekend is enough to make one good thing

Two days, a wheel each, and the studio’s clay. You leave with the pieces worth keeping; we fire them, and you come back for them.

  1. Saturday morning: wedging, centring, and the first pull. Most people fail this one and then stop failing it.
  2. Saturday afternoon: throw until one of them is worth keeping. Everything else goes back in the bucket.
  3. Sunday morning: trim the foot, and sign the base while the clay still takes a mark.
  4. Sunday afternoon: glaze, then hand it over to the kiln and go home with clay in your hair.
