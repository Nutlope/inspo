1The reader

Nobody reads a design doc. Everybody skims one.

Your document is read in the gaps of someone else's day: ninety seconds before the meeting, a scroll during it, a search for their own team's name. Readers are not looking for prose. They are hunting for the decision, the cost, and what you need from them.

When a skimmer cannot find those three things, they do not conclude that your document is badly structured. They conclude that your thinking is. The proposal reads as unfinished, the author as unsure. The work was right, and the room never found out.

The fix is not writing more, and it is not asking busy people to read harder. It is structural. Put the argument where the skim lands: the title, the first sentence, the headings, the bold spine of the page. Skimming is not your document's failure mode. It is the medium. Primer teaches you to write for it on purpose.

Read only the bold text of this section. It still works. That is not an accident. It is week two.

2The method

Three moves, practiced until they are reflexes.

2.1

Put the decision first.

Conclusions open; evidence follows. If the reader stops after one paragraph, they stop informed. If they keep going, everything they read now has a point.

wasThis document explores several possible approaches to the retry problem.
nowRetries should move to the queue. Two teams are affected; the migration is a week.
2.2

Make the headings carry the argument.

A heading is a claim, not a category. Someone who reads only your headings should be able to repeat your reasoning in the hallway afterward.

wasBackground
nowWhy the queue backs up on Mondays
2.3

Cut until it is direct.

Hedges read as doubt. The passive voice hides the one thing reviewers need: who does what. Shorter is not simpler; it is harder, and it is the job.

wasIt could be argued that latency might potentially be improved somewhat by this change.
nowThis change cuts p99 latency. The benchmark is in section three.
Every example on this page is a specimen, not a template. You practice the moves on your own prose. Templates expire. Judgment compounds.

3The syllabus

Four weeks. Eight evenings. One document that finally lands.

Week one

The reader

Evening one

How technical documents are actually read: in meeting gaps, out of order, in search of a decision.

Evening two

Openings and summaries. The first hundred words, rewritten until they can carry the whole document.

Week two

The structure

Evening one

Ordering an argument. Headings that make claims. The shape a skim can follow without getting lost.

Evening two

Workshop: restructure a doc you brought. The cohort reads only your headings, then tells you your own argument back.

Week three

The sentence

Evening one

Cutting. Hedges, throat-clearing, and the passive voice that hides who does what.

Evening two

Workshop: line edits on your own prose, traded across the cohort and defended out loud.

Week four

The room

Evening one

Proposals and objections. Writing for the meeting that decides, and for the people who were not in it.

Evening two

Workshop: your revised document, read cold by someone seeing it for the first time. It stands, or it does not.

Every workshop runs on documents you brought. Nothing in Primer is a writing exercise. You leave with your real doc, rewritten.

4The format

Built to fit around a working week.

Length
Four weeks. Long enough to change habits, short enough to finish.
Rhythm
Two evenings a week: eight live sessions, each half teaching and half workshop.
Cohort
Twenty engineers. Small enough that every document gets edited in the room, in front of everyone.
Material
Your own writing. Design docs, RFCs, ADRs, proposals, postmortems: whatever you already have to produce.
Between sessions
Revision, not homework. You rewrite the same document as the weeks sharpen it.

Primer is for you if

  • You are a senior engineer, and the document is your main instrument of influence.
  • Your proposals are technically right and still get misread.
  • You write constantly, and no one has ever edited your prose in front of you.

It is not for you if

  • You want a grammar refresher or a style quiz.
  • You want templates to fill in rather than judgment to build.
  • You cannot bring real documents to work on.
Sessions are live and run in the evening, so the course sits beside your job instead of on top of it.

5Apply

Bring the doc that got skimmed.

The application asks who you are, what you write, and for one document you wish had landed better. That document becomes your material for the four weeks, and the version you leave with is the proof.
