Five screens, in the order you'd hit them.

Each step is the actual interface, not a diagram of it. Read the copy if you like, but the screens are the argument.

Step 01

Point it at a card and walk away.

Drop a folder, a memory card, or a phone into the bin. Reelframe reads every clip's timecode, camera, frame rate and audio channels while it copies, so by the time the transfer finishes the shoot is already sorted by day, camera and take.

Nothing is transcoded behind your back. If a clip needs a proxy to play smoothly, you're asked, and the original stays where you put it.

Step 02

Delete a sentence, lose the frames.

Every clip with speech gets a transcript. Select words the way you'd select text in a document and press delete — the timeline cuts with it, audio and video together, ripple already handled.

The panel on the right collects the ums, the false starts and the long breaths. Toggle a group on and Reelframe shows you exactly what it plans to remove before it removes anything.

Step 03

The timeline is where you argue with yourself.

Once the shape is roughed in, everything else stacks on top of it: b-roll over the interview, a music bed under both, titles on their own lane. Tracks snap to the beat markers you drop while listening, so cuts land on the music instead of near it.

Trim with the keyboard the whole time. J, K and L still do what they've done for thirty years, and every edit is a ripple or a roll depending on which side of the cut you grabbed.

Step 04

Match the shots before you grade them.

Two cameras never agree. Pick one shot you like, then paste its balance onto the rest of the scene — Reelframe matches white point and exposure first, so your creative pass starts from footage that already cuts together.

Wheels, curves and a scope that tells you when you've crushed the blacks. Drag the divider in the viewer to see before and after on the same frame.

Step 05

One timeline, every shape it needs to be.

Choose the destinations and Reelframe reframes for each one, keeping the subject in frame instead of cropping the centre and hoping. Review the reframe on any shot it wasn't sure about before the render starts.

Renders run in the background. Keep editing while the queue works, or hand the project file to someone else — it references your originals and carries every edit decision with it.

What the tour left out.

A tour that only shows the good parts isn't a tour. Here's where Reelframe is deliberately thin.

01
It is not a compositor.

Keying, tracking and 3D live somewhere else. Reelframe round-trips to them and takes the result back onto the timeline, but it won't pretend to replace them.

02
Collaboration is file-based, not live.

Two people can't drag clips in the same sequence at the same time. You hand over a project file, or you work on separate sequences and merge.

03
Transcripts are good, not perfect.

Accents, cross-talk and jargon still need a human pass. That's why cuts stage before they commit rather than applying the moment they're found.

04
There is no mobile editor.

You can shoot on a phone and import over the network. The editing happens on a desktop, with a keyboard, because that's where trimming actually gets fast.
